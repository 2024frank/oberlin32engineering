Something to point to

A finished project with your name on it, and a written record of what you built and what you learned, is worth more on a 3-2 engineering application than a list of interests. Projects will be chosen partly on whether they leave you with that.

Something that matters here

The other half of the decision will be whether the work does something for people at Oberlin. A project that solves a real problem on this campus is a better project, and a better story to tell.

Project cycle

Define, build, test, document.

Every team should leave behind enough evidence for the next group to understand the design and continue the work.

Define

Write the problem

Name the user or setting, the constraint, the evidence you need, and what is out of scope.

Prototype

Build the smallest test

Choose a version that can answer one important question before the team commits to a larger design.

Test

Record what happened

Save measurements, failures, photos, code, design files, and the decisions that changed the direction.

Share

Make the next step clear

Demonstrate the result, publish a short build note, and explain what another team should try next.

Propose a project

Define the problem and the first test.

A proposal can be technical, environmental, artistic, or accessibility-focused. Explain who it is for, what the first version should test, and what resources the work may require.

A complete team is not required. The proposal process can identify collaborators and missing expertise.
